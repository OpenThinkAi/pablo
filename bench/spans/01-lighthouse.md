# Chapter one

The light had been automatic since 1979 and nobody had told the gulls.

<<<span
Nan Corrigan climbed the eighty-two steps twice a day out of habit, not
duty, and she climbed them at low water because that was when the reef
showed and she liked to count the seals. Low water on the fourteenth was
at 6:12 in the morning and 6:40 at night, and she had the times written
on the back of an envelope taped above the sink, the way her father had
kept them, the way his father had.

Her brother Tom owed eleven thousand pounds to a man in Oban and had
owed it for two years. He had never said so directly. He had said things
around it, the way a man walks around a hole in a floor, and Nan had
learned to hear the shape of the hole from the walking.

At the top she opened the shutter and let the morning in. The glass was
salted over on the seaward side. She cleaned it anyway, though the lamp
inside it had not been lit by a human hand in forty-six years, and she
cleaned it in a spiral from the centre outward because that was the only
way she had ever seen it done.

"You'll wear it through," Tom said from the stairs. He was out of breath.
He had been out of breath since March and would not see anyone about it.

"It's an inch thick."

"It'll be an inch less when you're done."

She did not turn around. Below them the reef was coming up black and
streaming, and the seals were already on it, seven of them, which was
two fewer than the week before. She counted them twice to be sure and
then she said, without any particular weight, that she had been through
their father's papers and that the boat was in both their names.

Tom said nothing to that for long enough that a wave broke.

"It always was," he said.

"It was. I'm saying I've seen it written down now."
span>>>

Below, the tide turned and began the long business of coming back.
